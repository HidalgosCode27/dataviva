Mapping the Competition Before You Launch

A growing market isn't the same as an open one. Before a Valencian bike-helmet brand enters Bogotá, this piece maps who's already there — on price, on perceived innovation, and on the digital visibility that decides whether a new entrant gets found at all.

By Andrea Hidalgo Arce — Data & International Economics
Why this piece exists. This is adapted from a team market-internationalization project for a Valencian bike-helmet company entering Colombia, which I co-led together with Jade Campillos — Klaudia Kathi and Miguel Molina contributed additional sections of the broader report. This piece draws specifically on the competitive matrix built for the market-selection chapter (six brands scored across price, positioning, and digital presence), presented here as a standalone competitive-intelligence exercise, independent of the specific product.

01 — Size the opportunity firstA growing category, entered from the outside

Before mapping who's already competing, it's worth confirming the market itself is worth competing for. The global bicycle-helmet market was valued at $870M in 2025 and is projected to reach $1.2B by 2034 — a modest but steady 3.65% CAGR. That's not explosive growth; it's the kind of category where competitive positioning, not category tailwinds, decides who wins share.

02 — Price and perceived innovation don't move togetherWhere six brands actually sit

The original report's own positioning map plotted competitors on price vs. quality — a reasonable starting point, but one that collapses "quality" and "innovation" into the same axis. Splitting them out changes the picture: Closca and LIVALL both score highest on perceived innovation, but at radically different price points and through completely different mechanisms — design and sustainability for one, hardware and connectivity for the other. 03 — Share of voiceClosca isn't winning on price. It's winning on visibility

Of the six brands mapped, only three had a published, comparable follower count. Among those, Closca leads by a wide margin — not because it's the cheapest (it isn't) or the most technically advanced (LIVALL's Bluetooth-and-SOS system arguably is), but because its content strategy is built around lifestyle and aesthetics rather than product specs, a format that travels further on Instagram.

04 — Where the field is actually contestedNobody is paying for visibility yet

Across all six brands, none were running visible paid search (SEM) — the entire category is competing on organic presence. That's a real opening: SEO strength varies widely (Closca and B'TWIN — via Decathlon's domain authority — both score "Alto," everyone else trails), and a category with zero paid competition is one where an above-average SEO investment goes further than in a market where competitors are already bidding on keywords.

Worth noting. The original report contains a second, separate competitor analysis later in the document (BTWIN, Rockrider, Giro, Bunker, on a simple price-vs-quality map) built for a different chapter. This piece draws only on the earlier, more granular six-brand matrix above — the two aren't reconciled with each other in the source material, which is a genuine gap worth flagging rather than quietly merging.

What this translates to for market intelligence practice

  • Share of voice isn't market share. Leading on followers is a distribution advantage worth having, but it's a leading indicator to validate against sales data, not a substitute for it.
  • Collapsing "quality" and "innovation" into one axis hides real strategic differences. Two brands can both be "premium" while competing on entirely different capabilities — splitting the axes changes who you'd actually benchmark against.
  • An uncontested channel is a temporary advantage, not a permanent one. Zero SEM activity across a category is exactly the kind of signal that should trigger a "move first" recommendation, with a clock attached — competitors can start bidding on keywords at any time.
