The Real Cost of Crossing a Border: A Landed-Cost Case Study

A €89.90 product becomes a €95.00 landed price by the time it reaches a customer 8,000 km away — and the gap isn't where most cost models assume it is. Using a Valencia-to-Bogotá market-entry case, this piece breaks down what actually drives landed cost: packaging engineering, freight, customs friction, and margin stacking.

By Andrea Hidalgo Arce — Data & International Economics

01 — Before you price it, you have to fit itPackaging isn't a footnote — it sets the unit economics

Every landed-cost model starts with a physical question: how much product actually fits in a shipment? The product here is a 250g folding bike helmet, non-perishable, non-hazardous except for one detail that changes its shipping classification — an embedded emergency-positioning chip powered by a lithium battery, which requires UN 3481 hazardous-goods labeling regardless of how light or safe the product otherwise is.

Working from the individual box (25 × 15 × 20 cm) and a standard EPAL Europallet (120 × 80 cm, 144 mm base height), the optimal load is 8 boxes along the length and 3 across the width — 24 boxes per layer — stacked 7 layers high before exceeding a manageable pallet height. That's a calculation, not an estimate, and it's the number that everything downstream (freight cost per unit, container fill rate, per-unit logistics cost) gets divided by.

02 — Same product, two pricesMapping where the extra €5.10 actually goes

The domestic and export cost stacks share a starting point — €40 in production cost — and diverge from there. It's tempting to assume the gap is the import tariff. It isn't: in this build, the difference comes almost entirely from an extra logistics leg (inland transport, customs formalities, main freight) layered before the same 20%–20% margin structure and a comparable consumption tax.

A gap worth flagging. The published Colombian tariff schedule sets a 10% MFN duty on imported helmets, but the cost table above has no separate tariff line — only customs formalities (€2.50). That's either an intentional assumption (Spain–Colombia trade may qualify for preferential treatment under the EU–Andean trade agreement) or a gap in the original model. 03 — The friction a tariff line doesn't captureCustoms isn't binary — it's a performance score

Duties are a known, published number. Customs efficiency isn't — and it moves the real cost of clearing goods just as much. The World Bank's Logistics Performance Index scores Colombia's customs process at 2.21 out of 5 for 2023, ranking 84th of 139 countries assessed — and the original research traced a declining trend since a 2018 peak, ending 2023 close to the 2016 low. A low, unstable customs score means less predictable clearance times, which is a cost even when it never shows up as a line item: it shows up as safety stock, missed delivery windows, and inventory tied up in transit.

04 — Risk lives in the incoterm, tooWho owns the risk after the pallet leaves the dock

The model uses a DAP (Delivered at Place) incoterm rather than the more common DDP: the seller covers cost and risk up to an agreed point in Colombia, but the buyer — a local subsidiary, in this case — handles import clearance from there. That single choice moves customs risk, not just customs cost, off the exporter's books. Tap each stage below to see who's actually holding cost and risk at that point.

What this translates to for cost-to-serve and customs risk modeling

  • Palletization math is a cost input, not an afterthought. Units-per-pallet drives freight cost per unit directly — get the cubing wrong and every downstream margin calculation is wrong too.
  • Landed cost needs a line for every leg, verified against the actual tariff schedule. A missing or assumed duty line is exactly the kind of gap that turns a clean model into a pricing surprise post-launch.
  • Customs performance is a forecastable risk, not a fixed fact. An LPI-style score with a visible trend is a leading indicator for where safety stock and lead-time buffers actually need to go.
