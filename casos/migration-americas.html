When the Route Shifts: Reading Migration in the Americas

In 2024, irregular crossings fell sharply through the Darién Jungle, Honduras and the US southwest border, while irregular events in Mexico rose. The people didn't disappear; the corridor moved. A quarterly report built to give governments one regional picture to decide from.

Why this piece exists. This page revisits the quarterly report Migration Trends in the Americas (October–December 2024), published by the IOM Regional Office for Latin America and the Caribbean together with the Office of the Special Envoy for the Regional Response to the Situation in Venezuela. The report was designed as a decision-support tool for economic and migration policy: it consolidates official data from each country, key news and policy changes at the main points along the routes. It was produced jointly by Andrea Hidalgo and colleagues; the project was led by Andrea Hidalgo Arce, with graphic design by a colleague. The charts on this page are new, built from the report's published figures.

01 — Corridors, not countriesDown in three places, up in a fourth

Looked at one country at a time, 2024 reads as a year of decline. Looked at together, it reads differently. Crossings fell in Panama, Honduras and at the US southwest border, but irregular migration events in Mexico, counted only through August, had already passed the full-year 2023 total. The monthly average in Mexico more than doubled between the same eight-month windows, from 49,896 to 115,636 events (+132%).

02 — The legal channelsRegular pathways moved in opposite directions

Regular migration remains the main form of mobility in the Americas, but 2024 shows no common trend. Permanent residencies rose in Argentina (+53%) and Canada (+20%) and fell in the United States (−14%) and Chile (−12%). Temporary residencies were even more uneven, from +140% in Peru to −51% in Brazil.

The report also notes a policy change inside these numbers: on October 4, 2024, the US Department of Homeland Security announced that nationals admitted under humanitarian parole (Cuba, Haiti, Nicaragua, Venezuela) could not renew it after their two-year period ends.

03 — The human cost of transitFewer crossings, no fewer deaths on the record

IOM's Missing Migrants Project recorded 1,176 missing or dead migrants in the Americas in 2024, with Central America the most affected subregion, and more than 10,000 since 2014. Two regional records were broken: 341 lives lost in the Caribbean and 173 in the Darién National Park, the highest figures for both since 2014. Falling crossing numbers and rising fatalities in the same year are a reminder that volume and risk are separate measures.

The displacement picture inside countries is just as stark. The Americas registered 2.8 million internal displacements in 2024 (637,000 due to conflict and violence, 2.1 million due to disasters), about 5.9% of the global total. Haiti alone had 1,041,229 internally displaced people by December 2024, 48% more than in September.

04 — The economic tie that staysRemittances outweigh investment and aid

Migration is also an economic flow. Latin America and the Caribbean were projected to receive $136 billion in remittances in 2024, double the 2017 level. Mexico alone accounted for 40% of 2023 inflows. For Mexico, Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador and Nicaragua, 2024 remittances were six times foreign direct investment and eighteen times official development assistance.

What this translates to for policy and data analysis

  • Track corridors, not borders. A decline in one country's count can be a shift of the same flow to another. A regional view is the only one that can tell the difference.
  • Harmonize before comparing. The sources behind a regional report cut their data at different months and measure different things (documents issued, entries, encounters). The honest comparison states the cut-off beside every number.
  • Correlation isn't a mechanism. Irregular crossings fell in a year when several legal pathways tightened, but the data alone can't say whether one caused the other. A good brief names the pattern and stops there.
