Three Markets, One Decision: Why the Scorecard Didn't Win

Score Japan, Singapore and Colombia against the same ten market-entry criteria, and Colombia comes out lowest of the three. It's also the market a Valencian bike-helmet brand chose to enter. That gap between the matrix and the decision is the actual story — and the risk the team accepted to get there.

By Andrea Hidalgo Arce — Data & International Economics
Why this piece exists. This is adapted from a team market-internationalization project for a Valencian bike-helmet company, which I co-led together with Jade Campillos — Klaudia Kathi and Miguel Molina contributed additional sections of the broader report. This piece revisits the market-selection chapter's own ten-criteria comparison of three candidate countries, and adds a scoring layer the original report didn't build: turning each qualitative rating into a favorable / neutral / unfavorable call for market entry, tallied per country.

01 — Start with what's actually comparableThe hard numbers, before the qualitative ratings

Singapore leads on both dimensions by a wide margin — the wealthiest and most densely packed of the three, which is exactly the profile that made it the region's cycling-safety-regulation pioneer (helmets have been mandatory since 2019). Colombia trails on both. On these two numbers alone, it's the weakest candidate of the three.

02 — The original ten-criteria matrix, scored for favorabilityTurning Alto/Medio/Bajo into a decision

The source research rated all three countries across ten market-entry criteria — but never converted those ratings into a directional call. Some criteria are straightforwardly "higher is better" (purchasing power, sustainable consciousness). Others aren't: a "low" competitor density is favorable, not unfavorable, and lower social stratification widens the addressable market rather than narrowing it. On a strict count, Singapore wins (8/10), Japan is close behind (7/10), and Colombia trails clearly (4/10). If market selection were purely a scoring exercise, Colombia loses. It was chosen anyway.

03 — Why the low scorer wonWhat the matrix can't weigh

Three things don't show up as a clean "Alto" in a ten-row table, but drove the actual decision. First, scale of an existing habit: Bogotá already has over 600 km of bike lanes — more than any other Latin American capital — and roughly 900,000 daily bike trips, against Singapore's smaller, still-doubling network and Japan's cycling infrastructure that connects cities rather than serving them internally. Second, competitive space: Colombia was the only market rated "Bajo" on competitor density, meaning fewer established premium brands to displace. Third, momentum: Colombian e-commerce retail sales grew 21.6% year-over-year in Q3 2025 — a market still forming its digital retail habits, which is a very different entry environment than Singapore's already-mature, brand-saturated online market.

The risk they accepted knowingly. Colombia's Gini coefficient sits at 53.9 — high inequality that means a premium €95 product is reachable for a narrow slice of the population, not the median consumer implied by aggregate GDP figures. Compounding that: Colombia's 2013 trade agreement with the EU eliminates tariffs on EU-origin goods, but Closca manufactures in China — so the preferential access the market-entry case partly leans on may not actually apply without meeting the agreement's rules-of-origin requirements. Neither of these disqualifies Colombia; both should be named explicitly in the business case rather than smoothed into a "Media" rating.

What this translates to for market-intelligence practice

  • A scorecard is a starting point, not a verdict. The highest-scoring market and the right market aren't always the same one — but the gap between them needs to be argued explicitly, not skipped.
  • Aggregate ratings can hide the criterion that actually matters most. Competitor density and existing category infrastructure did more work in this decision than purchasing power did — weighting, not just scoring, belongs in the model.
  • Name the accepted risk in the business case, not just the opportunity. A Gini coefficient and a rules-of-origin question are exactly the kind of detail that should sit next to the recommendation, not get lost two chapters earlier.
