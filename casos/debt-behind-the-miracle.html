The Debt Behind the Miracle

South Korea turned a war-flattened economy into one of the world's most advanced in three decades. The same growth model now shows up in a different set of numbers: the highest household debt in Asia, the lowest fertility rate ever recorded, and a welfare state built for a country that no longer has the workforce to fund it.

By Andrea Hidalgo Arce — Data & International Economics

01 — A growth model running out of runwayFrom double-digit miracle to 1.4%

South Korea's post-war trajectory is a genuine outlier: GDP per capita rose from roughly $7,000 in 1990 to nearly $36,000 in 2024 — a five-fold increase. But the growth rate behind that transformation has been decelerating in clear, dateable stages, and 2023's 1.4% is the slowest of them.

The deceleration after 2012 isn't cyclical — it's structural, and it traces to a productivity split running through the entire economy. Overall labor productivity reached $100,000 per employee in 2024, below the $110,000 OECD average; inside that figure, SME manufacturing productivity sits at just 32% of large-firm levels, far under the OECD's 55% average. Nearly 5% of GDP goes to R&D — among the highest rates globally — but it concentrates in export-oriented chaebols and never fully diffuses into the small-business layer where most people actually work.

02 — Debt as the release valveThe highest household leverage in Asia

When wage growth and productivity gains slow, consumption has to come from somewhere else. In South Korea's case, it came from household borrowing — reinforced by the jeonse lease system, which requires tenants to pay a massive lump-sum deposit instead of monthly rent, effectively forcing households into debt just to secure housing.

The threshold matters as much as the level: research cited in the original study puts the point where household debt starts actively depressing private consumption at 80–84% of GDP — households above that line prioritize debt service over spending. South Korea has been above it for years, and its target of bringing the ratio back down to 80% remains, by the government's own framing, one of the country's central economic challenges.

03 — The demographic clock underneath it all0.75 children per woman, and a welfare system built for a different pyramid

South Korea's fertility rate hit 0.75 births per woman in 2024 — not a dip, but the continuation of a decline that crossed the replacement threshold back in 1983. The 2023 population pyramid shows why the next decade is the hinge point: the largest cohort sits between 45 and 60, the generation that built the modern economy and is now approaching retirement, supported by a shrinking base beneath it.

The strain shows up unevenly. Employment reached a historic high of 62.7% in 2024, but South Korea also carries the OECD's largest gender pay gap (29%) and a 17.2-point gap in labor force participation between men and women — a pattern the original research read as employers routing women into precarious, "irregular" work rather than closing the gap directly. At the other end of the age curve, elderly poverty stands at 40.4%, the highest in the OECD, while the national relative poverty rate has been stuck near 14.8% for a decade — above Italy or Germany.

04 — One institution, two bad optionsThe Bank of Korea's policy trap

Household debt and demographic decline meet at the central bank's door. Cutting interest rates would ease debt service and could reactivate consumption — but risks reinflating the housing prices that drove the debt in the first place (prices already rose 32% in two years post-COVID, peaking in June 2022). Holding rates high avoids that risk, but chokes an already-weak domestic demand further, deepening the consumption drag the debt threshold describes.

Cut rates

Eases household debt service, could revive consumption — but risks reinflating housing prices and rebuilding the same debt overhang.

Hold rates high

Avoids a new housing bubble — but suppresses domestic demand that's already weak, deepening the consumption drag from existing debt.

What this translates to for policy and economic analysis

  • A country's headline growth story and its structural risk story are often two different data sets. The five-fold GDP-per-capita increase and the 93.5% debt ratio are both true at once — a diagnostic has to hold both.
  • Cross-source reconciliation is part of the analysis, not a footnote. BIS, IMF and OECD figures on household debt don't always agree exactly — knowing which source measures what, and why, changes how confidently a number can be used.
  • A policy dilemma is more useful stated as a trade-off than as a recommendation. The Bank of Korea's rate choice has no clean answer — naming both risks explicitly is more defensible than picking a side the data doesn't clearly support.
