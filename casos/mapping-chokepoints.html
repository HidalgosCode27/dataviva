Mapping Chokepoints: A Supply Chain Risk Case Study

How do you quantify the risk of a single-supplier dependency before it turns into a production stoppage? Using South Korea's semiconductor exports as the test case, this piece builds a structural vulnerability framework — and a scorecard — for supply chains where one blocked input can shut down billions in output.

By Andrea Hidalgo Arce — Data & International Economics
Why this piece exists. I originally built this analysis for a graduate research project on global value chains. I'm adapting it here because the underlying question — how exposed is an operation when it depends on a handful of irreplaceable inputs — is the same question any supply chain analytics function has to answer, whether the product is a memory chip or a shipping lane. The method is the point: structured risk scoring from trade data, not the semiconductor industry itself.

01 — Concentration as a leading risk indicatorRevenue concentration is a risk metric, not just a growth story

Before mapping any single point of failure, the first question is exposure: how much of the total output rides on the thing that could fail? Semiconductors grew from roughly 10% to 21.6% of South Korea's exports between 2010 and 2024 — the same metric a supply chain function would track as revenue-at-risk if it monitored dependency on a single supplier, lane, or SKU category.

The pattern that matters here isn't the growth trend — it's the volatility around it. Every spike lines up with a demand shock (the 2017–18 memory "supercycle," the 2024 AI-server boom); every dip lines up with a sector contraction. A concentrated revenue base doesn't just grow faster — it swings harder, which is exactly why concentration belongs on a risk dashboard, not only a growth one.

02 — Specialization ≠ flexibilityStructural strength can mean structural rigidity

The Revealed Comparative Advantage index stayed between 4.2 and 5.6 for fifteen straight years — well above the threshold of 1.0 that signals specialization. That consistency is good news for competitiveness and bad news for optionality: it means this isn't a temporary bet that can unwind on its own. Any mitigation has to be engineered, not waited out.

05 — Risk isn't hypotheticalEvery row on that table has already failed once

A scorecard is only useful if it's grounded in what actually happened, not just what could. All four inputs were disrupted at least once in a five-year window:

July 2019
Japan removes South Korea from its preferential export whitelist for hydrofluoric acid and related chemicals.
February 2022
Russia's invasion of Ukraine halts the country's two main neon purification plants — roughly 70% of global supply.
2023
The US and the Netherlands tighten export licensing on advanced lithography systems to select destinations.
August 2023
China imposes export controls on gallium and germanium; gallium prices rise 68% within the year.

None of these were triggered by the company or the country holding the exposure — each came from a third party's policy decision. That's the operating condition a supply chain risk function actually has to plan for: disruptions it can't prevent, only anticipate and price in.

What this translates to for supply chain analytics

  • Concentration metrics belong next to revenue metrics. A share-of-total indicator (like the 21.6% figure) is cheap to track and flags where a single failure has outsized impact.
  • Substitutability and recovery time turn a supplier list into a risk-ranked one. The same four-column logic used here scales to a procurement or logistics dataset — supplier, concentration, substitutability, time-to-recover.
  • Incident history is a leading indicator, not a footnote. A chokepoint that has already failed once is more likely to fail again under stress; a scorecard that logs incidents turns a static risk map into a monitoring tool.
